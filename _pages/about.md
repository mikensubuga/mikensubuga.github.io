I am a PhD student at the [University of Bristol](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/persons/mike-nsubuga), funded by the MRC GW4 BioMed2 Doctoral Training Partnership, carrying out my doctoral research across Bristol and [Imperial College London](https://profiles.imperial.ac.uk/m.nsubuga). I work at the intersection of genomics, machine learning and public health, with a focus on foodborne pathogens and antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

**At Bristol**, I study how foodborne *Salmonella* Enteritidis spreads across the UK and how antimicrobial resistance emerges and moves within it. Working with the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA, formerly Public Health England), I use the UK's largest genomic surveillance dataset for this pathogen to trace how lineages and resistance spread between regions over time, and build machine learning models that predict where an infection is likely to have come from. This kind of source attribution helps public health teams find the origin of outbreaks quickly and target interventions where they will have the most effect. *Supervised by [Dr Sion Bayliss](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/persons/sion-c-bayliss); co-supervised by [Prof Kristen Reyher](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/persons/kristen-k-reyher), [Prof Andrew Dowsey](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/persons/andrew-dowsey) and [Dr Lauren Cowley](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/persons/lauren-cowley).*

**At Imperial**, I benchmark machine learning models that predict antimicrobial resistance from bacterial genomes. I use [CABBAGE](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/54/16/gkag780/8769244), the largest AMR genotype–phenotype database of its kind, to compare learning methods and ways of representing genomes, and to find which approaches are most accurate and reliable across pathogens and drugs. Identifying which models can be trusted is a step towards using genome sequencing for rapid diagnostics, treatment decisions and surveillance. *Supervised by [Prof Leonid Chindelevitch](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/people/l.chindelevitch); co-supervised by Dr Romain Derelle.*

Previously, I completed my MSc in Bioinformatics at Makerere University under the NIH-funded EANBIT programme. Death rates from AMR are highest in sub-Saharan Africa, yet African genomes are under-represented in public databases, so most prediction models are built on data from Europe and North America. My dissertation examined how well machine learning models that predict AMR in *E. coli*, trained on genomes from England, perform on genomes from three sub-Saharan African countries: Uganda, Nigeria and Tanzania ([BMC Genomics, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-024-10214-4)). Knowing where such models can and cannot be relied on shows where more data or better methods are needed; as resistance spreads across countries through travel, trade and the food chain, closing these gaps matters for global AMR surveillance.

I have also worked as a Research Data Scientist at Bristol's Jean Golding Institute (UK) and as a Research Fellow at the Infectious Diseases Institute's African Centre of Excellence in Bioinformatics (Uganda).
